Community Knowledge: What Local Fishers Taught Us About Their Coastline

By Shamin Rownok·Community stories

Every community-consultation session we run starts the same way: we come in expecting to explain our project, and we leave having learned something that changes it.

It's tempting, when you have satellite data and soil samples, to treat local knowledge as a nice-to-have — a courtesy conversation before the "real" science happens. In our experience, it's the opposite. The people who fish, farm and travel a stretch of coastline every day hold information no dataset captures: which creeks still run fresh after a storm surge, where mangrove seedlings used to take before a particular embankment went up, which plots flood first when the tide is unusually high.

What we actually ask

  • How has this stretch of coast changed in the last 10–15 years, in the resident's own words?
  • Which areas are used for fishing, boat access, or firewood — and which restoration plans would need to work around that?
  • What has been tried here before, and what happened to it?
  • What would make a restoration effort actually useful to this community, not just to the ecosystem?

That last question matters more than it might seem. A restoration site that ignores how people already use the land tends to get undone — trampled, repurposed, or simply neglected — however ecologically sound the planting plan was on paper.

Turning conversations into design decisions

Everything gathered in these consultations feeds directly into the restoration-planning phase of our pipeline. If a community flags a creek mouth as important for fish nursery habitat, that shapes species selection. If residents point out an informal grazing path through a candidate site, that shapes where planting actually happens, and how it's protected in the early years.

This is also where we're most careful about privacy — we document what we learn, but we keep exact household locations and personal details out of anything we publish, including this website.

Why this is the part we're proudest of

Satellite models and soil data can be learned from a textbook. What can't be learned that way is trust — and trust is built one conversation, one visit, one follow-up at a time. If this project leaves anything behind beyond restored canopy, we hope it's a working example of restoration done with communities, not around them.
